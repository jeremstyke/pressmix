How Often Should You Change Your Engine Oil?

How often should you change engine oil? It depends on your car, oil and driving. Here is how to find the right interval, check the level and spot warning signs.

The right oil change interval is the one set by your car's manufacturer, which you will find in the owner's manual or service book, often expressed in miles or kilometres and in months, whichever comes first. Modern cars using synthetic oil often go longer between changes than older ones, and hard driving conditions may call for more frequent changes. Here is how to know what applies to your car.

Why engine oil matters

Engine oil lubricates the moving parts of the engine, reduces friction, helps cool the engine and carries away dirt and deposits. Over time, oil breaks down and becomes contaminated. Old oil protects the engine less well, which can lead to faster wear and, in the worst cases, serious damage.

Where to find the right interval

The most reliable source is your car's owner's manual or maintenance schedule. It tells you:

  • the interval in distance and time;
  • the type and grade of oil to use (for example 5W-30);
  • any specification the oil must meet.

Many recent cars also have a service indicator on the dashboard that calculates when the next oil change is due based on how you drive.

Old rules like "every 3,000 miles" no longer apply to most modern cars, but your manual has the final word.

Why the time limit matters too

Even if you drive very little, oil ages. Moisture and fuel can build up, especially with short trips where the engine never fully warms up. That is why manufacturers set a maximum time between changes, often a year, even when the distance has not been reached.

When to change oil more often

Many manuals list a severe service schedule with shorter intervals. It often applies if you regularly:

  • make many short trips;
  • drive in stop-and-go city traffic;
  • tow a trailer or carry heavy loads;
  • drive in very hot, cold or dusty conditions;
  • idle for long periods.

A lot of everyday driving falls into these categories, so check which schedule fits you.

How to check your oil level

Checking the oil level between services is quick and important.

  1. Park on level ground and, unless your manual says otherwise, wait a few minutes after switching the engine off.
  2. Pull out the dipstick, wipe it clean, push it fully back in and pull it out again.
  3. The oil should be between the minimum and maximum marks.
  4. If it is low, top up with the oil recommended in your manual, a little at a time.

Some cars have an electronic oil level reading instead of a dipstick. Check about once a month and before long trips, at the same time as you check your tire pressure.

Warning signs

Contact a mechanic if you notice:

  • the oil pressure warning light on the dashboard: stop safely and switch off the engine;
  • the oil level dropping quickly between checks;
  • oil leaks under the car;
  • a burning oil smell or blue smoke from the exhaust;
  • unusual engine noises.

Should you do it yourself?

Changing oil yourself is possible if you have the right tools and know-how, but you must dispose of used oil responsibly at a collection point. Many drivers prefer a garage, which also replaces the oil filter and can spot other issues. Keep receipts to prove the car's service history, which matters for warranty and resale. Regular maintenance costs belong in your monthly budget.

Summary

Change your engine oil at the interval in your owner's manual, respecting both the distance and the time limit, and follow the severe schedule if you mostly do short trips, city driving or towing. Use the recommended oil, check the level monthly and see a mechanic if a warning light or leak appears.
