How to Create a Strong Password That Is Easy to Remember

Learn how to create a strong password that is easy to remember with the passphrase method, why length matters and how a password manager and 2FA protect you.

Published on November 10, 2026 · 3 min read

Contents

  2. The passphrase method
  3. Why you should never reuse passwords
  4. Use a password manager
  5. Turn on two-factor authentication
  6. Good habits to keep
  7. Summary

A strong password is long, unique and hard to guess. The easiest way to create one you can remember is to use a passphrase: several random words strung together. Then use a different password for every account, store them in a password manager and turn on two-factor authentication. Here is how to do it in practice.

What makes a password strong?

Attackers use software that tries huge numbers of combinations and lists of leaked passwords. A strong password resists these attacks because it is:

  • long: length is one of the most important factors, and many security guidelines now favor longer passwords over complex short ones;
  • unpredictable: no names, birthdays, pet names or common words on their own;
  • unique: never reused on another site.

Classic weak passwords include "123456", "password", a keyboard pattern like "qwerty" or your first name followed by a year.

The passphrase method

A passphrase is easier to remember and usually stronger than a short, complicated password.

  1. Pick four or more random words that have no obvious link, for example: lamp, river, orange, bicycle.
  2. Put them together, with separators if you like: lamp-river-orange-bicycle.
  3. Add a number or symbol if a site requires one.

The key word is random. A famous quote, song lyric or a sentence about your life is easier to guess. Some password managers can generate random passphrases for you.

Why you should never reuse passwords

When a website is hacked, stolen passwords are often tested on other services. This is called credential stuffing. If you use the same password everywhere, one leak can open your email, bank and social media accounts.

Your email password deserves special care: whoever controls your inbox can reset most of your other passwords.

Use a password manager

Nobody can remember dozens of unique passwords. A password manager solves this:

  • it generates long random passwords;
  • it stores them in an encrypted vault;
  • it fills them in automatically on the right sites, which also helps you avoid fake phishing pages.

You only need to remember one strong master password, ideally a passphrase. Many browsers and phones include a built-in manager, and dedicated apps exist too.

Turn on two-factor authentication

Two-factor authentication (2FA) adds a second step after your password, such as a code from an authenticator app, a security key or a text message. Even if someone steals your password, they cannot log in without this second factor.

Turn it on first for your email, bank, social networks and cloud storage. Some services now offer passkeys, which let you sign in with your phone or fingerprint instead of a password.

Good habits to keep

  • Never share your passwords, even with someone claiming to be from support.
  • Be wary of emails or messages asking you to log in through a link.
  • Change a password immediately if a service reports a breach.
  • Keep your devices up to date. Updates fix security holes, as explained in why is my phone so slow.

The same logic applies to crypto: read what a crypto wallet is to understand why a recovery phrase must stay offline. And once your accounts are secure, back up the photos on your phone.

Summary

Create strong passwords with a passphrase of several random words, use a different password for every account and let a password manager remember them. Turn on two-factor authentication for important accounts, especially your email, and never share your passwords with anyone.
