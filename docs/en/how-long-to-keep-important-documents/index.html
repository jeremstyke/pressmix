How Long Should You Keep Important Documents? A Practical Guide

Tax returns, pay slips, bills, bank statements: how long should you keep important documents? A guide to what to keep, what to shred and how to stay organized.

How long you should keep important documents depends on their type and on the rules in your country. As a general approach: keep identity and life documents forever, keep tax records for at least as long as the tax authority can review them, keep pay slips and pension records for a very long time, and keep everyday bills and receipts for a shorter period. Here is a practical way to sort your papers.

Why the rules differ

There is no single international rule. Retention periods are set by national laws on tax, employment, consumer rights and property, and they can change. The guide below gives common good practices, but always check the official advice of your tax authority and government services. When in doubt, an accountant or legal advisor can confirm what applies to you.

Documents to keep for life

Some papers are difficult or impossible to replace and should be kept permanently, in a safe place:

  • birth, marriage, divorce and death certificates;
  • passports and identity documents (including expired ones, which can help prove your history);
  • adoption papers and family records;
  • diplomas and certificates;
  • wills and important legal judgments;
  • military service records, where relevant.

Work and pension documents

  • Pay slips and employment contracts: many people keep them until retirement, as they can help prove your career history and check your pension rights.
  • Pension statements and retirement records: keep them for life.
  • Proof of unemployment or other benefits: keep them until your situation and any related pension rights are settled.

Tax documents

Tax authorities can usually review past returns for several years, and this period may be longer in some situations. Keep:

  • your tax returns and tax notices;
  • proof of income, deductions and credits you claimed;
  • records related to investments and property sales.

Check how many years your tax authority can go back, and keep your records at least that long, plus a safety margin.

Housing documents

  • Property deeds and purchase documents: keep them as long as you own the property, and often longer.
  • Records of major works and renovations: keep them while you own the home, since they can matter when you sell.
  • Rental contracts and inventories: keep them until the end of the lease and the return of your deposit, then a while longer in case of dispute.
  • Rent receipts: keep them for a reasonable period after you move out.

Everyday finances

  • Bank statements: keep them at least until you have checked them, and longer if they support tax claims.
  • Loan documents: keep them until the loan is repaid and you have proof it is fully settled.
  • Utility bills and phone bills: often a year or more, especially if you need proof of address.
  • Receipts for valuable items: keep them for the duration of the warranty and for insurance purposes.

Clear records also make it easier to follow a monthly budget.

Health and insurance

  • Medical records, vaccination records and test results: keep them for life if possible.
  • Insurance policies: keep them as long as they are active, plus the period during which a claim can still be made.

How to organize your papers

  1. Create a few clear folders: identity, work, taxes, housing, banking, health, insurance.
  2. Scan important documents and store copies securely, with strong passwords.
  3. Keep originals of irreplaceable documents in a fireproof box or safe place.
  4. Once a year, sort and shred what you no longer need.

When traveling, carry copies of key documents, as suggested in our carry-on checklist. If you work for yourself, record keeping is even more important: see how to start freelancing.

Summary

Keep identity, family, diploma and pension documents for life, tax records for at least as long as your tax authority can review them, and everyday bills for a shorter period. Rules vary by country, so check official sources. Organize papers in clear folders, scan them and shred what is no longer needed once a year.
