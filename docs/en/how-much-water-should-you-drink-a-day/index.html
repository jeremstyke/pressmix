How Much Water Should You Drink a Day? What the Guidelines Say

Is the 8 glasses a day rule true? See how much water you should drink a day according to health authorities, what changes your needs and signs you drink enough.

Health authorities give reference values of around 2 to 2.7 litres of total water a day for adult women and 2.5 to 3.7 litres for adult men, depending on the source. These figures include the water in food, which often provides a significant share. In practice, most healthy adults can rely on their thirst and the color of their urine, and drink more when it is hot or when they exercise.

What the official guidelines say

Two widely used references:

  • The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) sets adequate total water intakes of 2.0 litres a day for women and 2.5 litres a day for men.
  • The US National Academies set adequate intakes of about 2.7 litres a day for women and 3.7 litres a day for men.

Important: these are total water intakes, from both drinks and food. Fruits, vegetables, soups and dairy products all contain water. That is why the amount you actually need to drink is lower than these totals.

Is the "8 glasses a day" rule true?

The idea of drinking eight glasses of water a day is easy to remember, but it is not a strict scientific rule. It can be a reasonable benchmark for some people, but needs vary widely from one person to another. Other drinks such as milk, tea and even coffee also count toward your fluid intake.

What changes how much you need

Your water needs go up or down with:

  • Heat and humidity: you lose more water through sweat.
  • Physical activity: exercise increases losses, especially long or intense sessions.
  • Body size: larger people generally need more.
  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding: needs increase.
  • Illness: fever, vomiting or diarrhea cause extra water loss.
  • Age: older adults may feel less thirsty and need to pay closer attention.

Simple signs you are well hydrated

You do not need to count every glass. Watch for:

  • Pale yellow urine: a good sign. Dark yellow urine often means you need more fluids.
  • Rarely feeling very thirsty.
  • Regular trips to the bathroom during the day.

Signs of dehydration include thirst, dark urine, headache, tiredness and dizziness. Seek medical help if symptoms are severe, especially in young children and older people.

Can you drink too much water?

Yes, although it is uncommon. Drinking very large amounts in a short time can dilute the sodium in your blood, a condition called hyponatremia. It mostly concerns endurance athletes who drink excessively during long events. For everyday life, drinking according to thirst is usually enough. People with heart or kidney conditions should follow their doctor's advice on fluids.

Easy tips to drink enough

  • Keep a reusable bottle with you and refill it during the day.
  • Drink a glass of water with each meal.
  • Add flavor naturally with lemon, cucumber or mint.
  • Eat water-rich foods such as fruits, vegetables and soups.
  • Drink a bit more before, during and after exercise. If you are new to sport, see how to start running as a beginner.
  • Limit large amounts of fluid just before bed if you wake up at night to use the bathroom; our guide on how to fall asleep faster has more sleep tips.

Water, sugary drinks and alcohol

Water is the best everyday choice. Sugary drinks add calories and sugar without extra benefit, and alcohol can increase fluid loss. Tea and coffee in moderate amounts can count toward your daily fluids.

Summary

Adults need roughly 2 to 3.7 litres of total water a day depending on the source, sex and lifestyle, including water from food. The 8 glasses rule is only a rough benchmark. Let thirst guide you, check that your urine stays pale yellow, and drink more in hot weather, during exercise or when ill. If you have a medical condition, ask your doctor.
