How Much Screen Time Is OK for Kids? Guidelines by Age

How much screen time should kids have? See the main recommendations by age, why content quality matters and practical rules for healthy screen habits at home.

Published on November 10, 2026 · 3 min read

  3. Signs screen time may be too high
  4. Practical rules for a healthy balance
  5. Lead by example
  6. Summary

For young children, health organizations recommend little or no screen time before age 2 (video calls with family aside) and about one hour a day of quality content between 2 and 5, ideally watched with an adult. For older children and teens, there is no single number: the key is a consistent family plan that protects sleep, physical activity, homework and time together.

What the main guidelines say

Recommendations vary a little between countries and organizations, but they point in the same direction.

  • Under 2 years: the World Health Organization advises no sedentary screen time for children under 2, and the American Academy of Pediatrics also recommends avoiding screens other than video chatting at this age.
  • 2 to 5 years: the WHO suggests no more than one hour a day, and less is better. The American Academy of Pediatrics also recommends limiting use to about one hour of high-quality programs, ideally shared with a parent.
  • 6 years and older: rather than a strict limit, pediatric organizations encourage parents to set consistent limits and make sure screens do not replace sleep, exercise and other healthy activities.

Your country's health authorities may publish their own advice, and your pediatrician can help you adapt it to your child.

Why quality matters as much as quantity

Not all screen time is equal. One hour of video calls with grandparents, a creative app or an educational program shared with a parent is very different from hours of autoplay videos alone.

Ask yourself:

  • Is the content suitable for my child's age?
  • Is my child active or passive while using it?
  • Are we watching or playing together, and talking about it?
  • Does it replace sleep, outdoor play, reading or family meals?

Signs screen time may be too high

  • Trouble falling asleep or tiredness during the day.
  • Irritability or tantrums when screens are turned off.
  • Less interest in playing, reading or seeing friends.
  • Homework or chores regularly pushed aside.

If you notice these signs, it is a good time to adjust the family rules.

Practical rules for a healthy balance

Create screen-free zones and times

  • No screens during meals.
  • No screens in the bedroom at night: devices charge outside the room.
  • Stop screens about an hour before bedtime to protect sleep.

Make a family media plan

Agree on rules together: when, where and how long screens can be used. Older kids are more likely to respect rules they helped create. Write them down and put them on the fridge.

Use the tools available

Most phones, tablets and consoles include parental controls to set time limits, filter content and approve downloads. They are useful, but they do not replace conversations about what children see online.

Offer alternatives

Kids reach for screens when they are bored. Keep books, board games, art supplies and outdoor activities easy to access. A family walk or even a beginner run together can become a new routine.

Lead by example

Children copy what adults do. If parents are always on their phones, rules will feel unfair. Putting your own phone away during meals and family time sends a strong message.

On long journeys, a little extra screen time is fine: just plan downloads in advance, as suggested in our carry-on packing checklist.

Summary

Avoid screens other than video calls before age 2, keep to about one hour of quality content a day from 2 to 5, and set consistent family limits for older kids. Focus on content quality, protect sleep and family time, use parental controls and set a good example yourself. Your pediatrician can help if you have concerns.
