A side stitch is a sharp pain just under the ribs, usually on the right side, that appears while running. It is officially called **exercise-related transient abdominal pain (ETAP)**, and it is harmless. To get rid of it, **slow down to a walk**, **breathe out slowly and deeply**, and **press firmly on the painful spot** while bending slightly forward. To prevent it, avoid eating or drinking a lot in the **2 hours before running**, warm up gradually and work on your breathing rhythm.

## What is a side stitch exactly?

ETAP is a localised pain, often sharp or stabbing when intense and cramp-like or pulling when mild. Studies on runners show that:

- it is very common: a large share of regular runners experience it at least occasionally;
- it is **more frequent in young people** and less frequent with age and experience;
- it appears mostly in sports with **repetitive torso movement**, such as running and horse riding, and much less in cycling;
- it usually disappears within minutes after stopping.

## Why it happens: the main theories

Scientists still debate the exact mechanism. The most supported explanation is **irritation of the parietal peritoneum**, the membrane lining the abdominal wall. Friction between this membrane and the organs, increased by a full stomach and the bouncing of running, would trigger the pain. Other contributing factors:

- **A full stomach**: a meal or a large volume of drink before running is the most consistent trigger in studies. Sugary and concentrated drinks (fruit juice, some sports drinks) are worse than water.
- **Starting too fast**: a sudden jump in intensity, without warm-up, is a classic cause.
- **Shallow breathing**: breathing quickly and from the top of the chest increases tension on the diaphragm area.
- **Posture**: runners with a more rounded upper back (thoracic kyphosis) seem more prone to it.
- **Downhill running and strong impacts**, which increase organ movement.

## How to make it go away during a run

When the stitch hits, try these steps in order:

1. **Slow down** to an easy jog or a brisk walk. Do not stop abruptly if you can avoid it.
2. **Breathe out slowly and completely**, through pursed lips, as if blowing through a straw. Long exhalations relax the abdominal area.
3. **Press firmly** with two or three fingers on the painful point, and bend your torso slightly forward. Hold for 20 to 30 seconds.
4. **Stretch the side**: raise the arm on the painful side above your head and lean gently to the opposite side, holding for a few breaths.
5. **Change your breathing rhythm**: if you always exhale on the same foot, switch to a 3-step pattern (inhale for 2 steps, exhale for 1, or inhale for 3 and exhale for 2). This alternates the foot that lands at the end of each exhalation.

In most cases the pain fades in **one to a few minutes**. You can then pick the pace back up gradually.

## How to prevent side stitches

### Watch what and when you eat

- Eat your last **real meal 2 to 3 hours** before running.
- If you need a snack, keep it small (a banana, a few crackers) and take it at least **45 to 60 minutes** before.
- Drink **small sips** rather than large gulps, before and during the run.
- Avoid fruit juice, fizzy drinks and very sugary drinks just before.

Being well hydrated during the day matters more than drinking a lot right before running: see [how much water you should drink a day](/en/how-much-water-should-you-drink-a-day/).

### Warm up and start slowly

Spend **5 to 10 minutes** at an easy pace or with dynamic movements before speeding up. A progressive start lowers the risk considerably. Our guide on [how to warm up before exercise](/en/how-to-warm-up-before-exercise/) gives a simple routine.

### Train your breathing

- Practise **belly breathing** at rest: hand on your stomach, inhale so it rises, exhale slowly.
- During easy runs, use a **3:2 rhythm** (inhale for 3 steps, exhale for 2) or whatever feels natural, as long as you do not always exhale on the same foot.
- Run at a pace where you can still speak in short sentences, especially as a beginner.

### Strengthen your core

Some studies suggest a stronger trunk reduces stitches. Two or three sessions a week of **planks, side planks and dead bugs** (3 sets of 20 to 40 seconds each) are a good start.

## Special cases

- **Beginners**: stitches are more frequent in the first weeks. Alternate running and walking and increase gradually, as explained in [how to start running as a beginner](/en/how-to-start-running-as-a-beginner/).
- **Races**: adrenaline and fast starts make stitches more likely. Plan your breakfast 3 hours before and start a little slower than your target pace.
- **Left side vs right side**: the right side is more common, but a stitch on the left side has the same mechanism.

## When it is not a side stitch

See a doctor if:

- the pain **does not go away** after you stop exercising;
- it comes with **chest pain, shortness of breath** that is unusual for you, dizziness or pain spreading to the arm or jaw (call emergency services in that case);
- you have **fever, vomiting or pain at rest**;
- it appears after a fall or a blow.

## Common mistakes

- Drinking half a bottle of sports drink just before the start.
- Starting every run at race pace.
- Holding your breath or breathing in short gasps when the pain appears.
- Stopping running altogether for weeks: stitches usually become rarer with regular training.

> **Tip:** note in a running log what you ate and drank before each run where you got a stitch. After three or four occurrences, a pattern usually stands out.

## Summary

A side stitch (ETAP) is harmless and very common, especially in beginners. To stop it, **slow down, exhale slowly, press on the spot and lean forward**. To prevent it, **avoid large meals and drinks in the 2 hours before running**, warm up, start gradually, vary your breathing rhythm and build a stronger core. If the pain persists at rest or comes with unusual symptoms, see a doctor.
