Most dogs that eat grass are **perfectly healthy**: grass eating is an extremely common behaviour, and in most cases dogs **do not vomit afterwards** and do not seem ill before. The likely reasons are **instinct, taste, boredom or a need for fibre**. It becomes worth a vet visit when your dog eats grass **frantically**, **vomits often**, has **diarrhoea, a loss of appetite or lethargy**, or when the grass may have been treated with chemicals.

## How common is it?

Grass eating is one of the most frequently observed behaviours in dogs. Surveys of dog owners have found that **a large majority of dogs eat plants at some point**, grass being the most common, and that **only a minority show signs of illness before eating it**. Equally important: **most dogs do not vomit** after eating grass. The image of the sick dog that eats grass to make itself throw up is the exception, not the rule.

Wild canids, such as wolves, also eat plant material. It is therefore considered a **normal behaviour** inherited from their ancestors.

## The main reasons dogs eat grass

### 1. Instinct and ancestral behaviour

One hypothesis is that wild canids ate plants to help **expel intestinal parasites**: the fibre wraps around worms and helps move them out. Even in a dewormed pet dog, the behaviour remains.

### 2. They simply like it

Many dogs choose **young, tender grass**, especially in spring, and some are picky about the type of grass. If your dog calmly grazes, chews and moves on, it is probably just taste and texture.

### 3. Boredom or excess energy

A dog left alone in a garden for hours, or a young dog that does not get enough exercise, may chew grass to pass the time. Grass eating that happens mostly when the dog is **alone or under-stimulated** points to this cause.

### 4. Need for fibre

Some dogs on low-fibre diets eat more grass. There are reported cases of dogs that stopped grazing once switched to a **higher-fibre food**. This is worth discussing with your vet before changing the diet.

### 5. An upset stomach

Some dogs with **nausea or digestive discomfort** gulp grass quickly and vomit shortly after. Here the behaviour is different: the dog seems agitated, swallows big mouthfuls without chewing, licks its lips, swallows repeatedly.

### 6. Attention seeking

If you react strongly every time your dog eats grass (shouting, running after it), it may learn that it is a great way to get your attention.

## Normal grazing vs warning signs

**Probably normal:**

- your dog chews grass calmly, a few blades at a time;
- it does it occasionally, during walks or in the garden;
- it eats, drinks, plays and has normal stools;
- it vomits once in a while at most, with no other symptom.

**See a vet if:**

- it eats grass **frantically** or suddenly much more than usual;
- it **vomits more than once or twice in a day**, or regularly over several days;
- you notice **blood** in the vomit or stools, or black stools;
- there is **diarrhoea, loss of appetite, lethargy, a swollen belly** or signs of pain;
- it tries to vomit **without producing anything** with a hard, swollen abdomen: this can be a sign of **gastric dilatation-volvulus (bloat)**, which is an emergency, especially in large deep-chested breeds;
- it also eats **soil, stones, fabric or other non-food items** (pica), which can point to a nutritional deficiency, a medical problem or a behavioural issue.

## The real risks of eating grass

The grass itself is rarely the problem. The risks come from what is on it or around it:

- **Pesticides, herbicides and fertilisers**: avoid treated lawns, golf courses and the edges of farm fields. If you treat your own lawn, follow the product's re-entry time and keep your dog off it.
- **Toxic plants mixed in with grass**: lily of the valley, foxglove, oleander, some bulbs. Know what grows in your garden.
- **Parasites**: grass contaminated by the faeces of other animals can carry worm eggs. Keep your dog's **deworming schedule** up to date.
- **Grass awns** (foxtails): those dry, arrow-shaped seed heads in summer can get stuck in the throat, nose, ears or between the toes. Sneezing fits, head shaking or a swollen paw after a walk require a vet check.
- **Long blades** occasionally cause gagging or a strand hanging from the back end: never pull hard on it, let the vet handle it if it does not come out easily.

## What to do if your dog eats too much grass

1. **Rule out a medical cause** with your vet if the behaviour is new, frequent or accompanied by any symptom above.
2. **Increase exercise and mental stimulation**: longer walks, sniffing games, puzzle feeders, short training sessions. A tired dog grazes less out of boredom.
3. **Review the diet** with your vet: a food with more fibre, or adding a small portion of cooked vegetables (green beans, pumpkin), may reduce the urge.
4. **Offer an alternative**: a safe chew toy or a pot of **dog-safe grass** (such as wheatgrass or oat grass) you grow yourself, free of chemicals.
5. **Redirect calmly**: teach a "leave it" cue and reward when your dog looks at you instead of grazing. Avoid chasing or shouting.
6. **Supervise in risky areas**: use a lead near treated lawns and fields.

## Is it the same for cats?

Cats eat grass too, and more often vomit afterwards, partly because grass helps them bring up hairballs. If your cat vomits often, our article on [why your cat is throwing up](/en/why-is-my-cat-throwing-up/) explains when to worry.

## FAQ

### Should I stop my dog from eating grass?

Not necessarily. If your dog is healthy and the grass is untreated, occasional grazing is fine. Just avoid chemically treated areas.

### My dog eats grass and then vomits yellow foam. Is that serious?

Yellow foam is usually **bile** from an empty stomach. Occasional episodes, often in the morning, can be linked to a long gap between meals. If it happens regularly, talk to your vet, who may suggest splitting meals.

### Can eating grass give my dog worms?

The grass itself does not, but grass contaminated by other animals' faeces can. Regular deworming protects your dog.

## Summary

Eating grass is a **normal and very common behaviour** in dogs, usually linked to **instinct, taste, boredom or fibre needs**. Most dogs do not vomit afterwards. Watch for **frantic grazing, repeated vomiting, diarrhoea, lethargy, a swollen belly or pica**, and keep your dog away from **treated lawns, toxic plants and grass awns**.
