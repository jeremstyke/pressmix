A **sinking fund** is money you set aside **a little every month for a specific expense you know is coming**: car insurance, Christmas presents, a holiday, new tyres, a laptop replacement. You divide the total cost by the number of months left, save that amount each month in a dedicated account or "pot", and when the bill arrives, the money is already there. It turns big, irregular expenses into small, predictable ones and stops them from going on a credit card.

## Sinking fund vs emergency fund

The two are often confused, but they have different jobs:

- **Emergency fund**: for the **unexpected**. Job loss, a medical bill, a broken boiler. You don't know when or how much. It typically covers **3 to 6 months of essential expenses** and should not be touched for anything else. See [how to build an emergency fund](/en/how-to-build-an-emergency-fund/).
- **Sinking fund**: for the **expected**. You know roughly how much and when. Annual subscriptions, birthdays, back-to-school costs, a car service.

The problem many people face is using their emergency fund (or their credit card) for expenses that were completely predictable. A Christmas that costs £600 every year is not an emergency: it is a sinking fund that was never created.

## How to calculate a sinking fund

The formula is simple:

**Monthly amount = (Target amount - Amount already saved) ÷ Number of months until the expense**

### Example 1: car insurance

- Annual premium: **$1,200**, due in 10 months.
- Already saved: $0.
- Monthly amount: 1,200 ÷ 10 = **$120 per month**.

### Example 2: Christmas

- Budget: **£600**, it is now January, so 11 months to go (until early December).
- Monthly amount: 600 ÷ 11 = **about £55 per month**.

### Example 3: replacing a laptop

- Your laptop is 3 years old and you expect to replace it in about 2 years.
- Expected cost: **€1,000**.
- Monthly amount: 1,000 ÷ 24 = **about €42 per month**.

If you start late, the monthly amount rises: the same Christmas budget started in June becomes 600 ÷ 6 = £100 per month. **Starting early is what makes sinking funds painless.**

## Step-by-step: how to set one up

### 1. List your irregular expenses

Go through the last 12 months of bank and card statements and note every expense that is **not monthly**. Typical categories:

- **Car**: insurance, annual service, tyres, road tax, registration, repairs.
- **Home**: home insurance, boiler service, small repairs, new appliances.
- **Subscriptions** paid yearly: software, memberships, streaming, delivery memberships.
- **Gifts and celebrations**: birthdays, Christmas or other holidays, weddings.
- **Travel**: summer holiday, visiting family.
- **Children**: school supplies, clothes, activities, school trips.
- **Health**: glasses, dental work not covered, vet bills for pets.
- **Tech**: phone and laptop replacement.

### 2. Estimate the amount and the date

For each item, write down the **expected cost** and the **due date**. Round up rather than down: prices rise, and a slight surplus is better than a shortfall.

### 3. Calculate the monthly contribution

Apply the formula to each line, then **add them up**. Do not be surprised if the total looks high: these expenses were always there, they were just hitting your budget all at once.

### 4. Choose where to keep the money

- **A separate savings account**, ideally easy-access, so you can withdraw without penalty.
- Many banks and apps offer **"pots", "spaces" or "buckets"** within one account: one pot per sinking fund keeps things clear.
- If your account pays interest, even better, but **accessibility matters more than yield** here. Avoid locking the money in a fixed-term account if the expense date is uncertain.
- Do **not** invest sinking fund money in shares or crypto: the expense date is close and the value could fall right when you need it.

### 5. Automate it

Set up a **standing order or automatic transfer** on payday for the total amount, then split it between pots. Automation is what makes sinking funds work: you don't have to think about it.

### 6. Spend from it, then reset

When the expense arrives, pay it from the sinking fund. Then recalculate for the next cycle. If you spent less than planned, keep the surplus there or move it to another goal.

## How it fits into your budget

Sinking fund contributions are a **budget line like rent or groceries**. In a [monthly budget](/en/how-to-make-a-monthly-budget/), they belong in your fixed or planned expenses. If you follow the 50/30/20 approach, contributions for needs (insurance, car repairs) go into the "needs" part, and those for wants (holidays, gifts) into the "wants" part.

If the total is too high for your current income:

1. **Prioritise** essential and legally required items (insurance, tax, repairs).
2. **Reduce** some targets (a cheaper holiday, a lower gift budget).
3. **Stretch** timelines where possible (keep the laptop one more year).
4. Look for savings elsewhere, for example on [groceries](/en/how-to-save-money-on-groceries/).

## Common mistakes

- **Creating too many small funds**: 20 pots of £5 is hard to follow. Group similar items (for example one "car" fund for insurance, service and tyres).
- **Borrowing from one fund for another** without paying it back.
- **Forgetting price increases**: insurance premiums and subscriptions tend to go up each year. Check last year's bill and add a margin.
- **Mixing sinking funds with the emergency fund** in the same account with no tracking.
- **Starting too late**, which makes the monthly amount too high and discouraging.

> **Tip:** name your pots after the goal ("Japan 2027", "New tyres") rather than "Savings 2". People save more consistently when the purpose is visible.

## FAQ

### How many sinking funds should I have?

Most households do well with **4 to 8**: car, home, gifts, holidays, subscriptions, children, tech, health.

### Should I build my emergency fund or sinking funds first?

Most advice suggests starting with a **small emergency buffer** first, then creating sinking funds for the biggest predictable expenses, and growing both in parallel.

### What happens if an expense comes earlier than planned?

Use what is in the fund and cover the gap from your current month's budget if you can, rather than the emergency fund. Then adjust the timeline for next time.

## Summary

A sinking fund is **planned savings for a known future expense**. List your irregular costs, estimate the **amount and date**, divide by the months remaining, and **automate** a monthly transfer into a separate account or pot. It keeps predictable expenses off your credit card and protects your emergency fund for real emergencies.
