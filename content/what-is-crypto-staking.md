Crypto staking means **locking up coins to help secure a proof-of-stake blockchain**, in exchange for **rewards** paid in that cryptocurrency. It is often compared to earning interest, but it is not a savings account: rewards vary, your coins can lose value, and there are technical and platform risks. Here is how it works.

## Proof of stake in simple terms

A blockchain needs a way to agree on which transactions are valid. Bitcoin uses **proof of work**, where miners use computing power. Many other blockchains, including **Ethereum** since 2022, use **proof of stake**.

In proof of stake:

1. **Validators** lock up (stake) a quantity of coins as a guarantee.
2. The network selects validators to **propose and confirm** new blocks.
3. Honest validators receive **rewards**.
4. Validators who break the rules or behave badly can lose part of their stake. This penalty is called **slashing**.

Staking is what gives validators "skin in the game" and keeps the network secure.

## Where do staking rewards come from?

Rewards usually come from **newly created coins** issued by the protocol and from **transaction fees**. The reward rate is not fixed: it depends on the blockchain, the total amount staked on the network and its activity. Any advertised rate is an estimate that can change.

Keep in mind that if new coins are issued, staking partly compensates for this dilution rather than creating pure profit.

## The main ways to stake

### Running your own validator

You run the software yourself. It gives you full control but requires technical skills, reliable equipment and, on some networks, a large minimum amount. Mistakes can lead to penalties.

### Delegating

On many blockchains, you can **delegate** your coins to a validator who does the work and shares the rewards, minus a commission. You usually keep the coins in your own wallet.

### Staking through an exchange

Many exchanges offer staking with one click. It is convenient, but the platform holds your coins (custody), takes a fee and you depend on its reliability. Availability also depends on local regulations.

### Liquid staking

Some services give you a **token representing your staked coins**, which you can use elsewhere. This adds flexibility but also extra risks linked to smart contracts and to the token's price.

## The risks to understand

- **Price volatility**: rewards are paid in crypto. If the price falls, you can lose money despite the rewards.
- **Lock-up periods**: on some networks, unstaking takes time, so you cannot sell instantly.
- **Slashing**: a validator's mistakes can reduce staked funds.
- **Platform risk**: an exchange or service can be hacked, fail or freeze withdrawals.
- **Smart contract risk**: bugs in liquid staking or DeFi protocols can lead to losses.
- **Scams**: be very wary of promises of high, guaranteed returns.
- **Taxes**: rewards may be taxable depending on your country.

## Is staking right for you?

Staking may suit someone who already plans to **hold a proof-of-stake coin for the long term** and understands the risks. It is not a way to get guaranteed income. Before considering crypto at all, build an [emergency fund](/en/how-to-build-an-emergency-fund/) and only use money you can afford to lose. For personal advice, consult a qualified financial professional.

To understand where your coins are kept, read [what a crypto wallet is](/en/what-is-a-crypto-wallet/), and secure your accounts with [a strong password](/en/how-to-create-a-strong-password/).

## Summary

Crypto staking means locking coins to help validate a proof-of-stake blockchain in exchange for variable rewards. You can run a validator, delegate, use an exchange or try liquid staking, each with different trade-offs. Rewards do not protect you from price drops, lock-ups, slashing, platform failures or scams.
