Cats often throw up because of **hairballs, eating too fast or a sudden change of food**. An occasional episode in a cat that otherwise eats, plays and behaves normally is usually not serious. But **frequent vomiting, blood, lethargy or refusal to eat** are signs that you should contact a vet quickly. Here is how to tell the difference.

## Vomiting or regurgitation?

It helps to know what you are seeing:

- **Regurgitation** is passive: undigested food comes back up shortly after eating, often in a tube shape, without much effort.
- **Vomiting** is active: the cat retches, its belly contracts, and the material may be partly digested, liquid or foamy.

Note what comes up, when and how often. This information is very useful for your vet.

## Common and usually harmless causes

### Hairballs

Cats swallow a lot of hair when grooming. Most of it passes through the gut, but some can build up and come back up as a **hairball**, often after some retching. Long-haired cats and cats that groom a lot are more affected.

### Eating too fast

Some cats gulp their food and bring it back up minutes later, sometimes whole. This is common in multi-cat homes where cats compete for food.

### A sudden change of diet

Switching foods abruptly can upset the stomach. Changes should be made **gradually over about a week**, mixing more of the new food each day.

### Eating grass or plants

Many cats nibble grass and then vomit. Be careful with houseplants and cut flowers: **some are toxic to cats**, lilies in particular.

## Causes that need a vet

Vomiting can also be a symptom of a medical problem, such as:

- **a swallowed foreign object** (string, elastic bands, small toys), which can be an emergency;
- **poisoning** from plants, human medication or household products;
- **intestinal parasites**;
- **food allergies or intolerances**;
- **digestive diseases** or other illnesses affecting the kidneys, liver or thyroid, more common in older cats.

Only a vet can identify these causes.

## Warning signs: when to see a vet

Contact a vet **promptly** if your cat:

- vomits **several times in a day** or keeps vomiting over several days;
- has **blood** in the vomit or vomit that looks like coffee grounds;
- seems **lethargic**, hides or appears to be in pain;
- **refuses to eat** for more than a day, or refuses to drink;
- has **diarrhea** at the same time;
- is losing weight;
- may have swallowed **a toxic product or an object**;
- is a **kitten, an elderly cat or has a known illness**.

Regular vomiting, even once or twice a week, is not normal and also deserves a check-up.

## What you can do at home

For a single episode in a cat that seems well:

1. **Watch your cat** for the next 24 hours.
2. Make sure fresh water is available.
3. Do not give human medication: many are dangerous for cats.
4. Ask your vet before changing the diet or withholding food.

## How to prevent vomiting

- **Brush your cat regularly** to reduce swallowed hair.
- Use a **slow feeder** or split meals into smaller portions.
- Change food gradually.
- Keep toxic plants, strings and small objects out of reach.
- Keep up with **deworming and vet check-ups**.

Vet visits can be expensive, so having an [emergency fund](/en/how-to-build-an-emergency-fund/) helps. Dog owners can also read [how often to bathe a dog](/en/how-often-should-you-bathe-a-dog/).

## Summary

Cats often vomit because of hairballs, fast eating or diet changes, and a single episode in a healthy cat is rarely serious. Repeated vomiting, blood, lethargy, refusal to eat or a swallowed object require a vet. Brush your cat, slow down meals and change food gradually to prevent problems.
