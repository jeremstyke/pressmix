When a child has a nightmare, the most helpful response is to **go to them quickly, reassure them calmly and briefly**, help them feel safe in their own bed, and **talk about the dream the next day** rather than in the middle of the night. Nightmares are **very common between 3 and 6 years old** and usually decrease with age. If they happen **several times a week for weeks**, follow a stressful event or seriously affect daytime life, talk to your doctor or paediatrician.

## Nightmare or night terror? Know the difference

The right response depends on what is happening. Parents often confuse the two:

**Nightmare**

- happens in the **second half of the night**, during REM (dream) sleep;
- the child **wakes up**, is frightened, **recognises you** and wants comfort;
- they can often **describe the dream**;
- they may have trouble going back to sleep.

**Night terror**

- happens in the **first hours of sleep**, during deep sleep;
- the child screams, sits up, may thrash or seem terrified, with eyes open, but **is not really awake**;
- they **do not recognise you** and may push you away;
- it lasts a few minutes, then they fall back asleep and **remember nothing** in the morning.

For a **night terror**, do not try to wake the child: stay nearby, make sure they cannot hurt themselves, and wait for it to pass. Night terrors are often linked to **sleep deprivation**, so an earlier bedtime frequently helps. Everything below is about **nightmares**.

## What to do in the moment

1. **Go to your child quickly.** Being alone with fear is what makes nightmares hard. A fast response teaches them they are safe.
2. **Stay calm and speak softly.** Your tone matters more than your words. A hug, a hand on the back, a quiet voice.
3. **Acknowledge the fear.** "That was a scary dream. You're safe now, I'm here."
4. **Remind them it was a dream.** For older children (5 and up), you can explain simply: "Dreams are pictures your brain makes while you sleep. It wasn't real."
5. **Keep it short.** Do not ask for every detail at 3am: it can fix the scary images in their mind. A few words are enough.
6. **Help them settle back in their bed.** Turn on a dim night light, give them their comfort toy, stay a few minutes. Taking them into your bed occasionally is not a disaster, but if it becomes a habit, it can be hard to undo.

## What to say (and what to avoid)

**Helpful phrases:**

- "I'm here. You're safe."
- "It was a dream. Dreams can't hurt you."
- "Let's think of something nice together: what do you want to dream about?"
- "I'll stay with you for a little while."

**To avoid:**

- "Monsters don't exist, stop being silly." Minimising the fear makes the child feel misunderstood.
- Checking under the bed or "spraying monster spray" every night: it can reinforce the idea that something might be there. Opinions differ, and it can work occasionally for young children, but don't turn it into a long ritual.
- Getting angry because you are exhausted: it adds stress to the fear.

## The next day: work on the dream

In daylight, a nightmare loses its power. You can:

- **Ask them to draw the dream**, then change the ending: the monster becomes small, funny or friendly. This technique, called **imagery rehearsal**, is used by professionals for recurring nightmares.
- **Make up a new ending together**, out loud, and repeat it at bedtime.
- **Read books** about fears and dreams, adapted to their age.
- **Look for a source**: a scary film, a video seen at a friend's, a conflict at school, a new sibling, a move.

## How to reduce nightmares

### Protect sleep quantity

Tired children have more disrupted sleep. As a guide, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends **10 to 13 hours (including naps) for 3 to 5 year-olds** and **9 to 12 hours for 6 to 12 year-olds**.

### A calm, predictable routine

The same sequence every night (bath, pyjamas, teeth, story, cuddle, light off) reassures. Plan **20 to 30 minutes** of calm time.

### Check what they watch

- No **frightening content** (films, news, games, videos) in the evening, and be careful during the day for sensitive children.
- **Turn off screens at least an hour before bed.** Our article on [how much screen time is right for kids](/en/how-much-screen-time-for-kids/) gives age-based limits, and these [screen-free activities](/en/screen-free-activities-for-kids/) are good wind-down ideas.

### Make the bedroom feel safe

- a **dim night light** if they ask for one;
- a **comfort toy** "on guard";
- door slightly open if it reassures them;
- a room that is not too warm.

### Talk about worries during the day

Many nightmares reflect daytime **stress**: school, friends, family tensions, separation. A regular moment to chat (at dinner, in the car) gives worries an outlet before bedtime.

## When to see a professional

Talk to your GP or paediatrician if:

- nightmares happen **several times a week for more than a few weeks**;
- your child **fears going to sleep** or refuses to sleep alone after being used to it;
- the nightmares started after a **frightening or traumatic event**;
- you notice daytime changes: **anxiety, irritability, school difficulties, bedwetting** coming back;
- there are signs of **sleep apnea**: loud snoring, breathing pauses, very restless sleep.

The doctor can rule out a medical cause and, if needed, refer you to a child psychologist.

## Common mistakes

- Asking for every detail of the dream in the middle of the night.
- Waking a child during a night terror.
- Letting them watch "just a little" of a scary film with older siblings.
- Pushing bedtime later, which increases fatigue and nightmares.

> **Tip:** keep a simple notebook for two weeks: bedtime, nightmares, what happened that day. Triggers often appear quickly.

## Summary

Nightmares are **common and usually harmless** in young children. Go to your child quickly, **reassure briefly and calmly**, help them settle back in their bed, and **talk about the dream the next day**, changing its ending through drawing or storytelling. Protect **sleep time**, keep a **calm routine**, avoid **scary content and late screens**, and see a doctor if nightmares are frequent, follow a trauma or affect daily life. Learn to tell them from **night terrors**, where you should not wake your child.
