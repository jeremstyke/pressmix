When the check engine light comes on, the car's computer has detected a fault in the **engine, emissions or fuel system**. If the light is **steady** and the car drives normally, you can usually keep driving carefully and **get the fault code read within a few days**. If the light is **flashing**, or the car loses power, shakes or smells of fuel, **slow down and stop as soon as it is safe**: a flashing light usually signals a serious **misfire** that can overheat and destroy the catalytic converter.

## What the check engine light actually is

The check engine light (also called the **malfunction indicator lamp**, MIL, or engine management light) is usually **orange or amber**, shaped like an engine outline. It is part of the **on-board diagnostics system (OBD2 in the US, EOBD in Europe)**, mandatory on petrol cars sold in the US since 1996 and in the EU since 2001 (diesels a few years later in Europe).

When a sensor reports a value outside the expected range, the engine computer (ECU) stores a **fault code**, such as **P0420** or **P0301**, and turns on the light. The light itself does not say what is wrong: only the code does.

## Steady vs flashing: the key difference

- **Steady light, car drives normally**: a fault has been recorded, but it is not an immediate danger. Book a diagnosis soon. Driving for weeks with it on can turn a cheap fix into an expensive one, and hides any new fault that would appear later.
- **Steady light with symptoms** (loss of power, rough idle, unusual noise, higher fuel use): reduce speed, avoid long trips and get it checked quickly. The car may have entered **limp mode**, which limits power to protect the engine.
- **Flashing light**: usually a **severe misfire**. Unburnt fuel goes into the exhaust and can overheat the **catalytic converter**, which is expensive to replace and can even become a fire risk. Reduce speed, avoid hard acceleration, and **stop and call for assistance** if the flashing continues.

**Red warning lights are a different matter**: oil pressure (oil can), engine temperature (thermometer) or battery/charging. A red oil or temperature light means **stop the engine as soon as it is safe**.

## The most common causes

### 1. A loose or faulty fuel cap (petrol cars)

A cap that is not clicked shut causes a fuel vapour leak that the evaporative emissions system detects. Tighten it until it clicks. The light will not go off immediately: it often takes **several driving cycles** (a few days of normal driving) to clear itself.

### 2. Oxygen (O2 / lambda) sensor

These sensors measure oxygen in the exhaust to adjust the fuel mixture. A faulty one increases fuel consumption and can damage the catalytic converter over time.

### 3. Spark plugs, ignition coils or injectors (misfire)

Codes P0300 to P0308 point to misfires, with the last digit indicating the cylinder (P0302 = cylinder 2). Worn spark plugs and failing ignition coils are very common causes. This is the typical "flashing light" case.

### 4. Catalytic converter

**P0420** (catalyst efficiency below threshold) is one of the most frequent codes. It can be the converter itself, but also a faulty sensor or an upstream problem (misfire, oil burning) that damaged it. Do not replace the converter without checking the causes first.

### 5. Mass airflow (MAF) sensor

A dirty or faulty MAF sensor causes hesitation, poor idle and higher fuel use. Sometimes a careful clean with a dedicated MAF cleaner is enough.

### 6. EGR valve and diesel particulate filter (diesels)

On diesels, a **clogged EGR valve** or a **blocked DPF** often triggers the light, especially on cars used mainly for short urban trips. A long drive at steady motorway speed can help the DPF regenerate, but if the DPF warning is also on, follow the manual and see a mechanic.

### 7. Vacuum leaks, sensors, wiring

A cracked hose, a faulty coolant temperature sensor, a corroded connector: many small faults can trigger the light.

## How to find out what's wrong

### Option 1: an OBD2 scanner

You can read the code yourself with an **OBD2 scanner**:

1. Find the **OBD port**, usually under the dashboard on the driver's side, near the steering column (16-pin trapezoid connector).
2. Plug in the scanner (wired) or a **Bluetooth OBD2 adapter** paired with a phone app.
3. Turn the ignition on (engine off or running, depending on the tool).
4. Read the stored codes and note them, including any "pending" codes.
5. Look up the code's meaning: generic codes (starting with **P0**) mean the same on all cars, while **P1** codes are manufacturer-specific.

Basic scanners and Bluetooth adapters are inexpensive and pay for themselves after one avoided diagnostic fee.

### Option 2: a parts shop or garage

Many auto parts shops read codes for free, especially in the US. Garages and dealers charge for a full diagnosis, which goes further than the code itself (tests, live data).

> **Tip:** a code tells you **which system** reported a problem, not always **which part** has failed. P0171 (system too lean) can be a vacuum leak, a dirty MAF sensor or a weak fuel pump. Avoid replacing parts just based on the code.

## Should you clear the code yourself?

A scanner lets you clear the code and turn off the light. Do it **only once the fault is fixed**, or to see whether it comes back. Clearing it without repairing anything:

- does not fix the problem;
- erases information useful to the mechanic;
- can cause a **failed emissions test or inspection**, because the car's readiness monitors are reset and need several days of driving to complete.

Disconnecting the battery to reset the light has the same downsides, and can also reset other systems.

## Can you pass an inspection with the light on?

In many places, **no**. In US states with OBD-based emissions testing, a lit check engine light is typically an automatic fail. In the UK, an illuminated **engine malfunction indicator lamp** is a MOT failure on cars where it is fitted. In EU countries with periodic technical inspection, it is generally recorded as a defect. Get it fixed before the test.

## Common mistakes

- Ignoring a steady light for months.
- Continuing to drive normally with a **flashing** light.
- Replacing the catalytic converter without fixing the misfire that killed it.
- Clearing the code just before an inspection.

## Summary

A **steady** check engine light means "get it checked soon": drive carefully and have the **OBD2 code** read. A **flashing** light or a car that loses power means **slow down and stop**: it is often a misfire that can ruin the catalytic converter. Start by checking the **fuel cap**, then read the code with a scanner or at a parts shop. Regular maintenance, such as timely [oil changes](/en/how-often-should-you-change-engine-oil/) and regular checks like [tire pressure](/en/how-to-check-tire-pressure/), keeps many of these warnings away.
