To start running as a beginner, **alternate short running intervals with walking**, three times a week, at a pace where you can still talk. Increase the running time gradually over several weeks rather than trying to run long from day one. This method builds endurance while keeping the risk of injury low.

## Before your first run

You do not need much to start, but a few checks help:

- **See a doctor first** if you have a heart condition, have been inactive for a long time, are significantly overweight or have joint problems.
- Get **comfortable running shoes**. A specialist running shop can help you find a pair that suits your feet.
- Wear light, breathable clothing suited to the weather.

That is all. No watch or app is required at the beginning.

## The walk-run method

Walk-run is the most beginner-friendly way to start. The idea is to **mix short running periods with walking breaks**, then slowly shift the balance towards running.

A typical first session looks like this:

1. **Warm up** with 5 minutes of brisk walking.
2. Run gently for **1 minute**, then walk for **2 minutes**.
3. Repeat this cycle 6 to 8 times.
4. **Cool down** with 5 minutes of easy walking.

Each week, if you feel good, increase the running intervals a little or shorten the walking breaks. Many beginners can run continuously for 20 to 30 minutes after a couple of months, but **everyone progresses at their own speed**.

## Find the right pace

The most common beginner mistake is running too fast. Use the **talk test**: you should be able to speak in short sentences while running. If you are gasping, slow down or walk.

Running slowly does not mean you are doing it wrong. Easy running builds the base that later lets you go faster and further.

## How often should you run?

**Three sessions a week** is a good starting point, with a rest day between runs. Rest is when your muscles, tendons and bones adapt to the new effort. On rest days, you can walk, cycle or stretch.

## How to avoid injury

- **Increase gradually**: add a little time or distance each week rather than making big jumps.
- **Listen to your body**: muscle soreness is normal, but sharp or lasting pain is a signal to stop and rest.
- Run on **varied, softer surfaces** such as trails or grass when you can.
- Add simple **strength exercises** like squats, lunges and calf raises once or twice a week.
- Sleep well: recovery happens at night, and our tips on [how to fall asleep faster](/en/how-to-fall-asleep-faster/) can help.

If a pain persists for more than a few days, see a doctor or physiotherapist.

## Stay motivated

- Set a **small, concrete goal**, such as running 20 minutes without stopping or finishing a local 5 km event.
- Run at the **same times each week** so it becomes a habit.
- Find a running partner or join a beginner group.
- Keep a simple log of your sessions to see your progress.

## Hydration and food

Drink normally during the day and have water available after your run. For short beginner sessions, you generally do not need sports drinks or gels. Our guide on [how much water you should drink a day](/en/how-much-water-should-you-drink-a-day/) gives useful benchmarks. Avoid a big meal right before running: leave a couple of hours after eating if you can.

## Summary

Start running with a walk-run plan three times a week, at a pace where you can talk, and increase your running time gradually. Invest in good shoes, rest between sessions and listen to your body. Patience is the key: steady progress beats doing too much too soon.
